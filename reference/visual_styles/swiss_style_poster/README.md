# Swiss Style Poster

## Overview
The International Typographic Style, known as Swiss Style, emerged in Zürich and Basel in the 1950s. Designers treated the poster as an information system: a modular grid, one sans-serif family, asymmetric balance and generous white space replaced illustration and ornament. Josef Müller-Brockmann's concert posters for the Tonhalle-Gesellschaft Zürich (from 1951) are a canonical example — pure geometric form repeated on a strict axis, with type set as a functional layer rather than decoration.

## Influenced By
- Bauhaus and De Stijl: modular construction, primary geometry and the rejection of ornament.
- New Typography (Jan Tschichold): asymmetric layout, sans-serif type and functional hierarchy.
- Constructivism: geometric abstraction placed in the service of public communication.

## Influenced To
- Corporate identity and wayfinding systems: grid-driven signage such as mass-transit diagrams.
- Later poster and editorial design: the grid as a visible organising principle rather than a hidden convenience.

## Relations
- [Soviet Constructivist poster](../soviet_poster/README.md): both build images from geometric primitives and strong diagonals; Swiss Style removes the political montage and keeps the measured grid.
- [1960s psychedelic poster](../psychedelic_poster/README.md): opposite strategies for attention — the Swiss poster controls the eye through hierarchy, the psychedelic poster overwhelms it.

## Style Requirements
- **Grid:** Snap every element to shared alignment edges; the underlying columns must be inferable from the layout.
- **Type:** Use a single sans-serif family in a small number of sizes, set flush left, with no decorative capitals or letterspacing tricks.
- **Palette:** Limit the image to two or three flat colors; keep maximum value contrast between figure and ground.
- **Geometry:** Build the focal element from pure primitives — circles, arcs, bars, squares — repeated or rotated on one axis.
- **Space:** Leave at least a third of the field empty; white space is a structural element, not a leftover.
- **Depth:** Avoid shading and perspective; allow overlap or scale alone to create layering.
- **Rhythm:** Produce motion through serial repetition with a controlled transformation, such as arcs rotating around a shared centre.
- **Photography:** If an image is present, crop it to a geometric edge and reduce it to high-contrast tones so it reads as a shape.
