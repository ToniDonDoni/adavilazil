# Goya's Disasters of War

## Overview
*Los Desastres de la Guerra* (*The Disasters of War*) is a series of prints Francisco Goya developed around 1810-1820 in response to the Peninsular War and its aftermath. The first edition of the eighty plates was published posthumously in 1863. The series moves from scenes of military violence to famine and, in its final group, allegorical criticism of the postwar political order.

The visual language is built from close, often unstable views of human suffering rather than heroic battle spectacle. Etched contours, aquatint and lavis tonal fields, drypoint and burin marks, and burnished highlights create abrupt shifts between exposed paper and dense shadow. This reference concerns the print series, not Goya's paintings or his other print cycles.

## Influenced By
- Goya's earlier printmaking, especially *Los Caprichos* (1799): an internal point of continuity in his use of etching, tonal printmaking and social criticism.
- European intaglio printmaking: Goya draws on established etching processes but combines etching, aquatint, lavis, drypoint, burin and burnishing in highly individual ways.

## Influenced To
- Later anti-war image-making: the series became a major reference for representing the human cost of conflict without heroic idealization.
- Pablo Picasso's *Guernica*: the Princeton University Art Museum describes Goya's prints as groundwork for later iconic representations of war, including *Guernica*. The Museo Reina Sofía also identifies Goya among the historical sources for Picasso's painting. This is an interpretive lineage, not a claim that Picasso copied the series' print technique.

## Relations
- [German Expressionist poster](../german_poster/README.md): both can use stark value contrast and distorted figures to create psychological force. Goya's series, however, builds narrative scenes through etched line and tonal printmaking rather than the simplified carved shapes of a poster.
- [Soviet Constructivist poster](../soviet_poster/README.md): both can make an image communicate a social or political position, but Goya's compositions are observational and narrative rather than built from geometric montage and typographic hierarchy.

## Style Requirements
- **Medium:** Reproduce an intaglio print character. Combine etched contours and hatching with aquatint or lavis tonal areas; use drypoint or burin accents sparingly.
- **Value:** Build a strong range from exposed paper to deep shadow. Let light isolate a figure, gesture or event without turning the image into polished cinematic lighting.
- **Highlights:** Use restrained burnished-looking highlights to pull selected forms out of dark passages.
- **Palette:** Keep the image monochrome or close to monochrome. Avoid decorative color.
- **Figures:** Show ordinary people as vulnerable, exhausted, defiant or grieving. Do not turn the scene into a heroic celebration of battle.
- **Composition:** Use close, compressed or abruptly cropped views when they intensify the event. Avoid a distant overview that makes individual suffering unreadable.
- **Space:** Let architecture, terrain and overlapping bodies create an unstable, crowded field. Keep the event legible even when the space feels chaotic.
- **Narrative:** Choose a specific human consequence of conflict, such as flight, injury, hunger, execution or mourning. Do not combine unrelated atrocities into generic spectacle.
- **Tone:** Preserve the series' critical, unsentimental stance. Avoid romanticizing violence or adding decorative horror effects.
- **Sequence:** If creating multiple images, vary the event and viewpoint while maintaining a consistent printmaking language. Do not repeat one dramatic composition as a template for every plate.
