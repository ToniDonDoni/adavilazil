# Georg Nees's Schotter

## Overview
Schotter (Gravel) is a computer-generated drawing by Georg Nees, dated 1968-1970. It consists of 264 equal-size square outlines arranged in 22 rows of 12. In the documented composition, the lowest rows remain close to a regular grid; moving upward, the squares receive progressively larger random rotations and positional displacements until the upper rows become irregular and begin to overlap. The drawing was generated with an ALGOL program on a Siemens 2002 computer and executed by a Zuse Graphomat Z64 plotter. The catalogued print is a screenprint after the plotter drawing. Its essential visual idea is a directional, controlled transition from order to disorder, not arbitrary noise. This reference follows the orientation in Frieder Nake's published reproduction and description: order at the bottom, disorder toward the top.

## Influenced By
- Max Bense's information aesthetics: the work treats composition as a generative procedure whose rules can be described and executed.
- Concrete and Constructivist art: the repeated geometry and ordered grid connect to the visual context of the early computer-graphics experiments shown in Stuttgart.

## Influenced To
- Later generative and algorithmic art: Schotter became a widely discussed example of how a small rule system can produce a complex visual progression.

## Relations
- Soviet Constructivist poster: both make geometric structure central, but Schotter derives its variation from programmatic perturbation rather than message hierarchy, photomontage or typography.

## Style Requirements
- **Grid:** For a faithful reconstruction, use 12 columns and 22 rows of equal-size square outlines in a portrait-oriented field.
- **Base order:** Keep the lowest rows aligned to a regular grid, with only negligible displacement or rotation.
- **Direction:** Increase positional and rotational variation progressively from the bottom toward the top. Do not reverse the direction.
- **Perturbation:** Use bounded random changes whose range grows with row position. Keep translation and rotation as distinct parameters rather than applying undifferentiated image noise.
- **Geometry:** Preserve each square's side length and shape. Vary its position and angle, not its scale or proportions.
- **Overlap:** Let larger deviations create occasional overlaps in the upper region while retaining recognizable square outlines.
- **Line:** Use thin, monochrome, plotter-like outlines with no fills, gradients or modeled lighting.
- **Background:** Keep the field plain and light. Do not add unrelated objects, textures or decorative elements.
- **Composition:** Make the progression from regular order to increasing disorder the sole dominant visual event.
