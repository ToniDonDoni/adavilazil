# Hiroshige's One Hundred Famous Views of Edo

## Overview
One Hundred Famous Views of Edo (Meisho Edo Hyakkei) is a late-career series of polychrome nishiki-e woodblock prints made by Utagawa Hiroshige I between 1856 and 1858. The series contains 118 designs by Hiroshige and follows the seasons while depicting celebrated places, urban activity and nearby landscapes. This reference describes recurring features of the series, not a rigid recipe that every print follows: unusual vantage points, oversized and cropped foreground motifs, layered distance, weather and seasonal atmosphere, and selective bokashi color gradation.

## Influenced By
- Edo-period meisho-e: the tradition of depicting celebrated places, seasonal landmarks and everyday local life.
- Ukiyo-e landscape and bird-and-flower printmaking: the series combines expansive views with close-up natural motifs and striking figure-ground relationships.

## Influenced To
- Vincent van Gogh: he copied two prints from the series, Sudden Shower over Shin-Ohashi Bridge and Atake and Plum Estate at Kameido, as oil paintings.
- Japonisme: the series was among the Japanese print sources that shaped late-nineteenth-century European interest in Japanese art; Van Gogh's copies are a documented example.

## Relations
- Levitan-inspired watercolor: both use season and weather to shape the mood of a landscape; Hiroshige relies on crisp woodblock contours, flat color planes and deliberate cropping rather than transparent washes and soft atmospheric blending.

## Style Requirements
- **Format:** Prefer a vertical oban-like composition. Treat the portrait format as a strong tendency, not an absolute rule for every image.
- **Foreground:** Use an oversized or sharply cropped foreground motif, such as a tree trunk, branch, bird, bridge, roofline or umbrella.
- **Framing:** Let the foreground interrupt, frame or partially conceal the distant scene. It may read almost as a separate view.
- **Perspective:** Choose an unusual high, low or oblique viewpoint. Favor asymmetrical framing and diagonals over centered one-point perspective.
- **Depth:** Separate foreground, middle ground and distance through overlap, scale and clear color planes.
- **Color:** Use distinct printed color areas and controlled, harmonious contrasts. Avoid making the image look like a softly blended watercolor.
- **Bokashi:** Apply selective graded color transitions in the sky, water or atmosphere where appropriate. Do not turn every surface into a digital gradient.
- **Weather and season:** Make rain, snow, wind, mist, twilight or seasonal change a visible part of the composition, not just background decoration.
- **Daily life:** Where appropriate, include small figures, boats, bridges or street activity integrated into the landscape.
- **Surface:** Preserve crisp contours and a restrained woodblock-print character. Avoid adding arbitrary grunge that obscures the image.
