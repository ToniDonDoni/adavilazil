# Hiroshige's One Hundred Famous Views of Edo

## Overview
*One Hundred Famous Views of Edo* (Meisho Edo Hyakkei) is a late-career series of polychrome nishiki-e woodblock prints by Utagawa Hiroshige I, published between 1856 and 1858. Hiroshige designed 118 prints; the complete set is commonly counted as 120 when the later contribution by Hiroshige II and the contents page are included. The series depicts famous places, ordinary activity and seasonal change in Edo and its surroundings.

This reference focuses on the series' unusually bold compositions: an enormous close foreground object can interrupt or frame a distant landscape; bridges, trees, birds and other motifs are cropped by the edge; and high or oblique viewpoints compress near and far space into a striking image. These are recurring devices, not rules that every print follows.

## Influenced By
- Edo meisho-e (famous-place pictures): the series continues the tradition of depicting celebrated locations, local life and seasonal landmarks.
- Earlier ukiyo-e landscape prints: Hiroshige develops the established woodblock landscape vocabulary through more radical foreground-to-background contrasts.
- Western linear perspective: the series selectively incorporates perspective devices into Japanese landscape printmaking; the University Art Museum at Tokyo University of the Arts identifies this combination as a defining compositional innovation.

## Influenced To
- Vincent van Gogh: he directly copied *Plum Estate, Kameido* and *Sudden Shower over Shin-Ohashi Bridge and Atake* as oil paintings.
- Japonisme and late nineteenth-century European art: the series became a source for artists exploring Japanese approaches to color, perspective and spatial structure.

## Relations
- [Levitan-inspired watercolor](../levitan_watercolor/README.md): both use season and weather to carry atmosphere. Hiroshige relies on crisp woodblock contours, flat printed color and deliberate cropping rather than transparent washes and soft watercolor edges.

## Style Requirements
- **Format:** Prefer a vertical oban-like composition, as in the series' common format.
- **Foreground:** Make one nearby object dramatically larger than the distant scene. It may be a tree trunk, branch, bird, bridge, roofline, banner or figure.
- **Cropping:** Allow the foreground motif to be cut by the image edge. It should feel deliberately close, not accidentally clipped.
- **Framing:** Use the foreground object to interrupt, frame or partly conceal the distant view. The two areas may read almost as separate pictures.
- **Viewpoint:** Choose an unusual high, low or oblique viewpoint. Avoid defaulting to a centered, eye-level panorama.
- **Depth:** Establish distance through overlap, scale and selective perspective rather than uniform atmospheric blur.
- **Color:** Use distinct, relatively flat printed color areas with a controlled palette. Deep indigo may carry water, night or distance.
- **Bokashi:** Apply graded color selectively in sky, water or atmosphere. Do not turn every surface into a smooth digital gradient.
- **Weather and season:** Make rain, snow, wind, mist, twilight or seasonal change visible in the composition.
- **Daily life:** When appropriate, include small figures, boats, bridges or street activity as part of the place being depicted.
- **Surface:** Preserve crisp woodblock contours and a printed-paper character. Avoid arbitrary grunge that obscures the drawing.
