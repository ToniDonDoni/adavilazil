# Visual Composition: Techniques and Rules

**Version:** 1.0.0

**Inspired by:** [https://youtu.be/osr259qQmHk?si=NuEvEN9BR9xtGFma](https://youtu.be/osr259qQmHk?si=NuEvEN9BR9xtGFma)

## Core Principle

Composition directs the viewer's attention. First decide what matters most, then design the visual path that leads the viewer toward it.

The source lesson contains ten main sections. Some sections describe several distinct techniques. The rules below retain those distinctions.

## 1. Rule of Thirds and Golden Ratio

### Rule of Thirds
- Divide the frame into a 3x3 grid.
- Position the primary subject near an intersection of the grid lines.
- Use off-center placement to create visual tension.
- Avoid centering every important element.

### Golden Ratio
- Use proportional relationships to organize elements harmoniously.
- Consider spiral arrangements to create a sense of visual flow.
- Use proportion as a structural guide rather than distributing elements arbitrarily.

## 2. Leading Lines

- Use roads, rivers, architectural elements, or light beams to guide the eye.
- Direct lines toward the primary focal point.
- Use converging lines to concentrate attention.
- Use vertical lines to create upward movement.
- Use diagonal lines to communicate motion and guide the viewer through the scene.

## 3. Negative Space and Visual Hierarchy

### Negative Space
- Leave empty space around important subjects.
- Use clean backgrounds to isolate the subject.
- Allow empty space to strengthen the subject's presence.
- Remove unnecessary visual details.

### Visual Hierarchy
- Establish one clearly dominant focal point.
- Avoid several equally bright or visually powerful elements competing for attention.
- Make secondary objects less prominent than the main subject.
- Ensure every visible element contributes to the composition's meaning.

## 4. Depth and Layering

### Three-Plane Composition
- Foreground: introduce a nearby detail, potentially blurred.
- Middleground: position the principal subject or action.
- Background: establish the environment and distance.
- Organize these planes to guide the eye progressively deeper into the image.

### Frame Within a Frame
- Place subjects inside natural or architectural frames.
- Use windows, doors, curtains, glass, or mirrors.
- Use overlapping frames to create additional depth.
- Create the impression that the viewer is witnessing a moment from within the scene.

## 5. Rhythm and Dynamic Tension

### Visual Rhythm
- Repeat elements to create a visual sequence.
- Repeated elements may vary in shape, size, or spacing.
- Use architectural structures, trees, windows, or other repeated forms.
- Arrange repetition to guide the viewer toward a focal point.

### Dynamic Tension
- Use diagonals instead of stable horizontal arrangements.
- Introduce intentional asymmetry.
- Shift elements away from balanced positions.
- Create energy by disrupting the viewer's expectation of visual stability.

## 6. Camera Angle and Shot Size

### Camera Angle
- Low angle: makes subjects appear larger, stronger, more imposing, or threatening.
- High angle: can communicate vulnerability, fragility, or submission.
- High angle can also create intimacy when looking down at a delicate object.

### Shot Size
- Close-up: emphasizes emotion, intimacy, and detail.
- Medium shot: emphasizes gestures, actions, and relationships between subjects.
- Wide shot: establishes environment, context, and surrounding space.
- Macro shot: transforms very small details into entire visual worlds.

## 7. Symmetry and Its Disruption

### Symmetry
- Use mirrored arrangements to convey order and stability.
- Center subjects when deliberate visual balance is required.
- Use symmetry to emphasize structured or artificial environments.

### Breaking Symmetry
- Introduce one deliberately misplaced element.
- Shift an object away from an otherwise symmetrical arrangement.
- Break an established pattern or rhythm.
- Use the disruption to attract attention and create dramatic tension.

## 8. Scale and Size Contrast

- Place very small subjects against enormous environments to emphasize scale.
- Use overwhelming surroundings to suggest vulnerability or insignificance.
- Alternatively, use a small isolated subject in a vast space to suggest strength or grandeur.
- Make an object occupy most of the frame when dominance is desired.
- Place dramatically different sizes together to create tension.
- Use size relationships to communicate the meaning of a scene.

## 9. Balance and Visual Weight

### Factors That Increase Visual Weight
- Faces attract more attention than ordinary objects.
- Movement attracts more attention than stillness.
- Bright areas attract more attention than dark areas.
- Warm colors tend to dominate cooler colors.
- Sharp angles attract more attention than smooth curves.

### Rules
- Identify which object should dominate the composition.
- Give that object the appropriate visual weight.
- Balance strong and weak elements intentionally.
- Use visual weight to establish a clear hierarchy of attention.

## 10. Color as a Compositional Tool

### Color and Depth
- Use warm colors in the foreground to create a sense of proximity.
- Use cool colors in the background to suggest distance.
- Adjust color temperature to change the perceived depth and atmosphere.

### Color and Attention
- Place a bright color accent at the intended focal point.
- Use a restrained background to strengthen that accent.
- Avoid distributing intense colors everywhere when one element needs to dominate.
- Use color deliberately to establish both hierarchy and mood.

## Overall Composition Workflow

1. **Define the primary subject and message.** What should the viewer understand?
2. **Choose the focal point.** Where should the viewer look first?
3. **Establish visual hierarchy.** What is primary, secondary, and background?
4. **Build the viewing path.** Use lines, rhythm, depth, and framing.
5. **Control visual weight.** Use scale, brightness, movement, and color.
6. **Remove distractions.** Keep elements that support the intended meaning.
