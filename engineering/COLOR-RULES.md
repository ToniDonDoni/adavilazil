# Color: Perception, Meaning, and Visual Rules

**Version:** 1.0.0

**Inspired by:** [https://youtu.be/1VRBrHb3TQU](https://youtu.be/1VRBrHb3TQU)

## Core Principle

Color directs attention, creates emotional associations, and changes the perceived meaning of an image.

Color is contextual. Its effect depends on surrounding colors, brightness, saturation, illumination, cultural associations, and the subject itself.

Choose colors according to the intended emotion and meaning, not just conventional associations.

## 1. Color Context and Contrast

- Evaluate colors against their surroundings.
- Consider the brightness difference between a subject and its background.
- Consider saturation and neighboring colors.
- Account for environmental illumination.
- Test important colors under different viewing conditions.

## 2. Authorial Interpretation

Before selecting a palette, answer three questions:

**1. What should the viewer feel?**
- Define the intended emotion and meaning.

**2. How should color be distributed?**
- Determine the amount, placement, and relationships between neighboring colors.

**3. What role should color play?**
- Decide whether color functions as a background, an accent, or an immersive environment.

## 3. Biological Color Signals

**Red**
- Use red when urgency, danger, or exceptional importance is intended.
- Consider red for warnings or critical actions.
- Avoid assuming that red always communicates the same emotion.

**Yellow and Black**
- Use strong yellow-black contrast for warnings and high visibility.
- Apply it selectively when immediate attention is required.

**Green and Blue**
- Consider green for associations with nature, growth, and environmental safety.
- Consider blue for calmness, openness, and stability.
- Check whether these associations fit the subject and audience.

These are associations discussed in the lesson, not universal physiological guarantees.

## 4. Cultural Color Codes

- **Red:** May communicate danger in Western contexts, celebration in Chinese contexts, or blessing in Indian contexts.
- **White:** May represent purity and weddings in some cultures, or mourning in others.
- **Yellow:** May communicate caution in Western contexts or historical imperial authority in China.
- Identify the intended cultural context before assigning symbolic meaning.
- Do not treat cultural associations as universal or unchanging.

## 5. Saturation and Color Dominance

**High Saturation**
- Use highly saturated colors to create an intense, expressive, or deliberately artificial world.
- Control the distribution of saturated colors rather than using them indiscriminately.

**Dominant Color**
- Reduce the palette to one dominant color when color itself should become the central experience.
- Use variations of that color to maintain visual interest.
- Avoid unnecessary competing hues.

## 6. Color Temperature and Immersive Light

**Color Temperature**
- Use warm and cool color relationships to influence the perceived atmosphere.
- Consider how temperature changes the apparent depth and distance of objects.
- Evaluate how the same scene feels under different lighting conditions.

**Color as Environment**
- Extend a dominant color across multiple surfaces and spatial layers.
- Coordinate light, background, objects, and atmosphere.
- Use this approach when the viewer should feel immersed in one emotional state.
- Treat colored illumination as part of the environment, not merely a decorative highlight.

## 7. Pattern Disruption and Meaning Fracture

- Identify the colors viewers normally associate with the subject.
- Deliberately introduce an unexpected color relationship.
- Preserve enough recognizable context for the contradiction to be understood.
- Use the mismatch to create tension, surprise, or curiosity.
- Avoid arbitrary unusual colors without a clear expressive purpose.

**Example principle:** A threatening situation presented in bright, cheerful colors can create tension through contradiction.

## 8. Total Color Immersion

- Choose one primary emotional state.
- Select a color associated with that state in the intended context.
- Extend the color through the environment, clothing, lighting, and surrounding objects.
- Use subtle tonal or saturation differences to preserve spatial readability.
- Maintain consistency when the goal is sustained emotional immersion rather than contrast.

## 9. Color as a Recognizable Visual Identity

- Use a consistent palette across related works.
- Associate recurring colors with a recognizable subject, experience, or meaning.
- Preserve sufficient consistency for the palette to become identifiable.
- Remember that recognition develops through repeated use and context, not from choosing a distinctive hue once.

## 10. Color Harmony

**Complementary Colors**
- Use opposing colors on the color wheel to create strong contrast and tension.

**Analogous Colors**
- Use neighboring colors to produce a cohesive palette with subtle variation.

**Triadic Colors**
- Use three separated color-wheel positions to balance color variety.

## Overall Color Workflow

1. **Define the intended emotion and meaning.** What should the viewer feel or understand?
2. **Identify the audience and cultural context.** Which associations are relevant?
3. **Choose the authorial strategy.** Reinforce expectations, disrupt them, or create total immersion.
4. **Select color relationships.** Define dominant colors, accents, saturation, and temperature.
5. **Distribute color deliberately.** Decide where each color appears and what it emphasizes.
6. **Evaluate color in context.** Consider neighboring colors, lighting, and visual hierarchy.
7. **Test alternative palettes.** Compare versions of the same image and evaluate changes in perception.
8. **Refine the result.** Remove colors that weaken the intended meaning.
